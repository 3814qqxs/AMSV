# The Commands of Messiah
### A Position Paper on the Great Commission and the Rule of Life for Disciples

**Summary.** The Great Commission does not merely instruct the assembly of Messiah to *make* disciples; it defines a disciple as one who is taught "to observe all things whatever I commanded you" (Matt. 28:20)[^matt2820]. Contemporary evangelicalism prioritizes disciple-making as a program but rarely follows the Great Commission directive connecting discipleship teaching and following an identifiable body of commands from the Lord Himself. This paper argues that the commands of Yeshua can and should be organized, and that this collection of instruction — promulgated first by Messiah directly, then confirmed and enumerated by the apostles, especially John — is the proper foundation of disciple-making.

But how does one go about organizing the teachings of Jesus into a coherent and consistent structure for the Church, while also maintaining the literal and historical truth of His earthly ministry?  The command to "go show yourself to the priests" given to the man healed of leprosy in Mark 1 is categorically different than the command to make disciples, even though both were true and literal historical events.  To fully understand the commands of the Messiah, and how they relate specifically to people living during His earthly ministry, and how they relate to all believers throughout history is an important distinction and one that must be included as foundational in systematizing commands given for disciple-making.  Jesus' earthly ministry can be segmented into two parts, divided by the hinge explored in Section III: Israel's leadership's formal, corporate verdict against Yeshua's messianic claim (Matt. 12:22–32)[^matt1231]. Section V will explore the commands given to the Church and those essential elements for following the Great Commission. 

That verdict changed the material intent of His ministry — teaching now veiled in parable, miracles now answering personal faith rather than offered as public national proof (§III.C) — but it did not end His engagement with Israel. Yeshua continued presenting Himself formally to the nation through the Triumphal Entry and the Temple controversies of Holy Week, continued calling her leaders to repentance, even while the nation's corporate judgment — Jerusalem's destruction in AD 70 — was already sealed (§III.D). What Messiah taught Israel under the kingdom offer, reaffirming and intensifying Torah, is catalogued in Section IV:

**Carried over from the Torah of Moses**
- Love the Lord your God with all your heart, soul, and mind (Matt. 22:37)[^matt2237]
- Love your neighbor as yourself (Matt. 22:39)[^matt2239]
- Honor your father and mother; do not murder, commit adultery, steal, or bear false witness (Matt. 15:4[^matt154]; 19:18–19[^matt1918])
- Worship and serve the Lord God only (Matt. 4:7, 10)[^matt47]

**Intensified beyond Moses**
- Anger toward a brother is treated as murder; be reconciled before you worship (Matt. 5:21–24)[^matt521]
- Lust is treated as adultery (Matt. 5:27–28)[^matt527]
- Love your enemies and pray for those who persecute you (Matt. 5:44)[^matt544]
- Do not resist the one who is evil (Matt. 5:39)[^matt539]

The second part is what Messiah gave specifically for the assembly age His own withdrawal opens (§III.C–D) — spoken in the Upper Room, after the resurrection, and at the Great Commission itself, then confirmed and enumerated by the apostles, especially John. That material, and the apostles' own restatement of it, is catalogued in Section V:

**Carried over from the Torah, as given to the church**, through apostolic restatement, not fresh direct address — love fulfills the whole law (Rom. 13:8–10)[^rom1389]; love of neighbor as "the royal law" (James 2:8)[^james28]

**Intensified, as given to the church**
- Forgive without limit (Matt. 18:21–22)[^matt1821]
- Whoever would be first must be servant of all (Mark 9:35[^mark935]; 10:43–44[^mark1043])
- Deny yourself and take up your cross daily (Luke 9:23)[^luke923]
- Love one another, even as I have loved you (John 13:34[^john1334]; 15:12[^john1512b])

**New to the Messianic economy**
- Repent and believe the good news; believe in Me (Mark 1:15[^mark115]; John 14:1[^john141])
- Be immersed in the name of the Father, Son, and Holy Spirit (Matt. 28:19)[^matt2819b]
- Keep My commandments; abide in Me (John 14:15, 21[^john1415b]; 15:4, 10[^john154])
- Do this in remembrance of Me (Luke 22:19)[^luke2219]
- Wait for the promised Spirit before you go (Luke 24:49[^luke2449]; Acts 1:4, 8[^acts14])
- Go and make disciples of all nations, teaching them to observe all I have commanded (Matt. 28:19–20)[^matt2819c]

**Confirmed and enumerated by the apostles**, most explicitly in 1 John and 2 John, and in James's and Paul's own marked distinction between what Messiah commanded and their own apostolic judgment (§V.G).

Beyond both parts, three further categories relate to, but do not belong to, the Law of the Messiah: textual example of practical living (concrete, authoritative, but addressed to a particular audience and circumstance — Messiah's own words at the wedding in Cana, John 2, and Paul's occasional instruction to Corinth alike), Christian liberty proper (Rom. 14; 1 Cor. 8–10; Gal. 5:13), and personal conviction (Spirit-given and binding on the individual conscience, but not universally legislated — cf. 1 Cor. 8:7). None of the three belongs to the Law of the Messiah, and none should be taught as universally defensible precepts for Christian living.

## I. The Great Commission and an Unasked Question

The Great Commission is usually cited for its participles — going, immersing, teaching — and its scope — all nations. Its grammar, however, carries a single imperative: *make disciples* (Matt. 28:19)[^matt2819]. Going, immersing, and teaching describe how that one command is carried out. The content of the teaching is specified precisely: "teaching them to observe all things whatever I commanded you" (Matt. 28:20)[^matt2820]. Disciple-making, in Messiah's own definition of it, is inseparable from a definite, teachable body of His commands.

This raises a question modern discipleship movements rarely ask directly: what, specifically, did Messiah command? The Old Covenant supplied Israel with an enumerable body of commands — traditionally reckoned at 613 — that could be taught, catalogued, and obeyed. The New Covenant, by contrast, is rarely treated the same way. Disciple-making is widely prioritized in evangelical circles today [the frequently repeated figure that roughly three-quarters of church leaders name it a top priority appears without a citation in the notes behind this paper and should be verified before it is used publicly], yet the *process* of making disciples varies widely from congregation to congregation, and is often built around curricula, spiritual disciplines, or leadership pipelines that are not derived from a direct accounting of Messiah's own words — often a mixture of leadership conviction promulgated with the weight of a command or imperative of Scripture. The result is a movement zealous for disciple-making but often more connected to a leadership style or cultural model than Scripture.

This paper attempts to organize Messiah's teaching, to bring to light the principles Scripturally defensible that all believers should live by. It begins with a framework for understanding how commands function across the biblical covenants (Section II), establishes the two-phase shape of Messiah's earthly ministry and why it matters for reading His commands (Section III), and then sorts His commands into the two parts the Law of the Messiah itself has: what He taught Israel under the kingdom offer (Section IV), and what He gave, directly and through apostolic restatement, for the assembly age (Section V). It then draws a boundary that requires great care to observe — not everything Messiah said or did in the Gospel narrative, and not everything the apostles wrote to a particular first-century congregation, functions as a command for the assembly in every age. Much of what passes for "discipleship" is instead textual example applied by analogy, Christian liberty exercised by faith in love, or individual conviction bound by conscience under the Spirit's own leading (Section VI). The paper closes by reconnecting this to the Great Commission itself (Section VII).

## II. The Law of the Messiah: A Framework

The framework for this paper is drawn from Arnold G. Fruchtenbaum's teaching "The Law of the Messiah" (Ariel Ministries), including his Fall Conference 2022 notes on the subject. Fruchtenbaum's argument runs, in brief, as follows.

Another comment relating to hermeneutic is warranted here as well; this paper is written from a literal textual framework, and follows the interpretation sequence for understanding Scripture:
1. Authorial intent
2. Meaning to the original audience
3. Internal biblical correlation
4. Application to the modern reader.

Frequently Scripture is misunderstood because this structure is inconsistently applied or there is a rush to application. However, only by placing the Scripture in its proper context can interpretative error be avoided.

The Torah of Moses — 613 commandments — was given specifically to Israel (Deut. 4:7–8[^deut47]; Ps. 147:19–20[^ps14719]; Mal. 4:4[^mal44]), not to the Gentiles and not to the Church. It was never a means of salvation (Rom. 3:20, 28[^rom320]; Gal. 3:11, 21[^gal311]) but a rule of life for a people already redeemed, given to reveal God's holiness and the standard of righteousness (Lev. 11:44[^lev1144]; 1 Pet. 1:15–16[^1pet115]), to mark Israel as distinct among the nations, and — per Paul — to expose sin and drive Israel to faith (Rom. 7:7[^rom77]; Gal. 3:19, 24–25[^gal319]). With the death of the Messiah, the Torah of Moses was rendered inoperative as a governing covenant (Gal. 2:16[^gal216]; 2 Cor. 3:2–11[^2cor32]; Eph. 2:14–16[^eph214]; Heb. 7:11–14[^heb711]) — a claim distinct from saying it was worthless; Paul calls it "holy, and the commandment holy, and righteous, and good" (Rom. 7:12)[^rom712].

In its place stands what Paul twice names directly: "the Torah of Messiah" (Gal. 6:2)[^gal62] and "the law of the Spirit of life" (Rom. 8:2)[^rom82]. Fruchtenbaum calls this the **Law of the Messiah** — the rule of life for New Testament believers from Acts 2 until the Rapture. It is, like the Torah of Moses, not a means of salvation but the evidence of love for the Messiah and of a Spirit-filled life (John 14:15[^john1415]; 1 John 5:2–3[^1john52]).

Fruchtenbaum offers four observations about how the Law of the Messiah relates to the Torah of Moses, which this paper adopts as its organizing categories:

1. **Common.** Some commands of Moses are repeated in the Law of the Messiah — nine of the Ten Commandments reappear in the New Testament's rule of life (the sabbath command does not).
2. **Different.** Some provisions are dropped or altered outright — dietary law is the clearest example.
3. **Amplified.** Some commands are carried forward but raised to a higher standard — love of neighbor becomes love "even as I have loved you" (John 15:12)[^john1512].
4. **New motivation.** The Old Covenant's logic was *obey in order to be blessed*; the New Covenant's is *obey because you have already been blessed* (cf. Eph. 1:3)[^eph13].

Sections III–V attempt to organize the teaching of Messiah into these categories, with one refinement the framework itself invites: because Messiah's own ministry does not speak with one undifferentiated voice throughout the Gospels. Section III establishes why.

## III. Two Phases of Messiah's Earthly Ministry: The Kingdom Offer and Its Withdrawal

Sections I–II establish that the assembly inherits a defined body of commands from Messiah, given force by apostolic restatement from Acts 2 onward. But not everything Yeshua said during His earthly ministry was spoken under identical conditions, and reading the Gospels as a flat, undifferentiated record of "instructions Messiah said" obscures the hinge the text itself marks plainly. Messiah's ministry falls into two phases, divided by Israel's leadership's formal verdict on His messianic claim — and what is commanded, how it is taught, and what evidence is offered for it changes on either side of that hinge.

**A. The Offer.** From His immersion through roughly the midpoint of the Galilean ministry, Yeshua's message is a straightforward offer: the kingdom of heaven is at hand (Matt. 4:17)[^matt417], the same announcement John the Immerser had already made (Matt. 3:2)[^matt32]. He teaches openly in the synagogues, plainly, without parables, and the offer is accompanied by public, verifiable, evidentiary miracles offered to the nation as a whole — healing "all manner of disease and all manner of sickness among the people," with the report going out "into all Syria" and crowds gathering "from Galilee and Decapolis and Jerusalem and Judea and from beyond the Jordan" (Matt. 4:23–25)[^matt423]. When John the Immerser, in prison, sends to ask whether Yeshua is "he that comes, or look we for another," Yeshua's answer is a public evidentiary list, not a private assurance: "the blind receive their sight, and the lame walk, the lepers are cleansed, and the deaf hear, and the dead are raised up, and the poor have good tidings preached to them" (Matt. 11:4–5)[^matt114] — Isaiah's own catalog of messianic-age signs (Isa. 35:5–6)[^isa355]. The mission during this phase is also deliberately restricted to Israel: the Twelve are sent "not into any way of the Gentiles, and... not into any city of the Samaritans: but... to the lost sheep of the house of Israel" (Matt. 10:5–6)[^matt105], preaching the identical message: "the kingdom of heaven is at hand" (Matt. 10:7)[^matt107]. The offer is real: the messianic kingdom, genuinely available to the nation, on the condition of accepting Yeshua as God's own Son, attested by public signs.

**B. The Hinge: the Unpardonable Sin.** Matthew 12 records Israel's leadership's formal verdict. When Yeshua casts out a demon and the crowd asks, "Can this be the son of David?" (12:23)[^matt1223], the Pharisees do not deny the miracle — they cannot — but supply an alternative account of it: "This man does not cast out demons, but by Beelzebub the prince of the demons" (12:24)[^matt1224]. Yeshua's response names the act for what it is. Distinguishing an offense against the veiled Son of man, who could be honestly mistaken, from attributing the Spirit's own manifest work to Satan, He states that the latter — a deliberate, informed rejection, not a private lapse available to any sinner unknowingly — "shall not be forgiven... neither in this world, nor in that which is to come" (12:31–32)[^matt1231]. This is the unpardonable sin: not an individual's unforgivable act available in every age, but Israel's leadership's official, deliberate verdict against Yeshua's messianic credentials, rendered on behalf of the nation they represented. Matthew 11 had already recorded the same rejection building — "Woe to you, Chorazin! woe to you, Bethsaida!... it shall be more tolerable for Tyre and Sidon in the day of judgment, than for you" (11:21–22)[^matt1121] — but 12:24 is where that rejection becomes an official, national verdict. Yeshua's own language for the nation shifts from that point: "this generation" becomes His marked designation for those under judgment — "the men of Nineveh shall stand up in the judgment with this generation, and shall condemn it... the queen of the south shall rise up in the judgment with this generation, and shall condemn it" (12:41–42)[^matt1241] — recurring through the rest of the Gospel until, in the last week of His life, "all these things shall come upon this generation" (23:36)[^matt2336].

**C. The Withdrawal: Parables and Faith-Conditioned Miracles.** "On that day" — the same day as the Beelzebub verdict — Yeshua goes out and, for the first time in Matthew's Gospel, teaches the crowds in parables (Matt. 13:1–3)[^matt131]. When the disciples ask why, His answer states the change in ministry method as a direct consequence of the nation's rejection: "To you it is given to know the mysteries of the kingdom of heaven, but to them it is not given... Therefore speak I to them in parables; because seeing they see not, and hearing they hear not, neither do they understand" (13:11–13)[^matt1311], fulfilling Isaiah's own commission to a people whose "heart has grown gross" (13:14–15, quoting Isa. 6:9–10)[^matt1314]. Parables now conceal the "mysteries of the kingdom" from a nation under judicial hardening while still disclosing them to those who receive Him — the same content, delivered in a different mode, adopted because the public offer has been officially refused.

The character of the miracles changes with it. Where the earlier miracles were performed publicly as evidence offered to the nation, the miracles that follow the rejection are repeatedly tied to, and limited by, an individual's own faith rather than offered as further public proof to a hostile leadership. In His own hometown, "he did not many mighty works there because of their unbelief" (Matt. 13:58)[^matt1358] — Mark's parallel states it still more starkly: "he could there do no mighty work, save that he laid his hands upon a few sick folk, and healed them. And he marvelled because of their unbelief" (Mark 6:5–6)[^mark65]. To the Canaanite woman: "great is your faith: be it done to you even as you will" (Matt. 15:28)[^matt1528]. To the two blind men: "According to your faith be it done to you" (9:29)[^matt929]. To the woman with the twelve-year hemorrhage: "your faith has made you whole" (9:22)[^matt922]. And when the Pharisees and Sadducees demand a sign a second time, He refuses outright: "there shall no sign be given to it, but the sign of Jonah" (16:1, 4)[^matt161]. Miracles do not cease; but the focus shifts from public, nation-directed proof to a personal transaction between Messiah and the faith of the one before Him.

**D. Postponed, Not Cancelled.** The withdrawal changes the material intent of Messiah's ministry, but it does not end His engagement with Israel, and it is not the nation's final word. He continues teaching in the Temple, answering the Pharisees, Sadducees, and a lawyer in turn (Matt. 22) — exchanges that stand as the culmination of His claim before the very leaders who had already rendered their verdict, not an afterthought to it. The Triumphal Entry itself is a further formal presentation: the crowd hails Him with the royal acclamation of Psalm 118 — "Blessed is the King that comes in the name of the Lord: peace in heaven, and glory in the highest" (Luke 19:38)[^luke1938] — the identical line Matthew 23:39 will hold out as the condition of His return. Yet at that same formal presentation Yeshua weeps over the city, because the nation's corporate judgment is already sealed even though not yet executed: "the days shall come upon you, when your enemies shall cast up a bank about you, and compass you round... and they shall not leave in you one stone upon another; because you knew not the time of your visitation" (19:41–44)[^luke1941] — fulfilled in Jerusalem's destruction by Rome in AD 70. Individual repentance remains genuinely open throughout — He keeps pressing it on Israel's leaders themselves, to the very end — but the nation's corporate verdict, rendered once at 12:24 by her authorized representatives, is not undone by any individual response to that continued appeal.

Even the same discourse that pronounces Jerusalem's house "left... desolate" (Matt. 23:38)[^matt2338] holds the door open: "you all shall not see me henceforth, till you all shall say, 'Blessed is he that comes in the name of the Lord'" (23:39, quoting Ps. 118:26)[^matt2339] — a national repentance not yet realized, and a promise that rejection is not cancellation. After the resurrection, Peter tells that same nation's rulers, in that same city, that repentance would still bring "seasons of refreshing from the presence of the Lord," and that God would "send the Messiah who has been appointed for you, even Yeshua," a Messiah "whom the heaven must receive until the times of restoration of all things" (Acts 3:19–21)[^acts319]. The kingdom offer is suspended pending Israel's national repentance, not withdrawn as though the promise itself had failed.

It is within this same withdrawal period — after 12:24, after the parables of chapter 13 — that Yeshua first names the entity that will carry His commands forward in the interval: "upon this rock I will build my assembly" (Matt. 16:18)[^matt1618], with the assembly's own disciplinary authority stated again at 18:17[^matt1817]. The congregation is not a contingency improvised after failure; it is what Yeshua begins to speak of, deliberately, from the moment the kingdom's public offer to Israel is withdrawn — occupying the interval Section II already named as the Law of the Messiah's own timeframe, Acts 2 to the Rapture.

**E. Why the Distinction Matters for This Paper.** This distinction is what allows Sections IV and V to exist as two separate catalogs rather than one undifferentiated list. What Yeshua taught Israel under the kingdom offer — Torah reaffirmed and intensified, most fully in the Sermon on the Mount — remains authoritative, but it reaches the assembly by a different route: not as direct original address to the coming Church, but through apostolic restatement after Pentecost (§V.A–B). What He gave after the withdrawal specifically for the age it opens — the Upper Room, the resurrection appearances, the Great Commission itself — is direct original address to that Church. The Great Commission's own charge — "teaching them to observe all things whatever I commanded you" (Matt. 28:20)[^matt2820b] — is given after the resurrection, after the withdrawal, after "my assembly" has already been named, to eleven men who will shortly receive the Spirit at Pentecost and open the Law of the Messiah's own era. Read against this section, "all that I have commanded" gathers both catalogs: what Israel was taught and the apostles carried forward (§IV, §V.A–B, §V.G), and what the assembly age received directly (§V.C–F). Sections IV and V survey each in turn.

## IV. Commands to Israel Under the Kingdom Offer

What follows is not exhaustive — a full catalog of every imperative in the Gospels would run well beyond a position paper — but it is representative, drawn from a survey of the Synoptics, of what Messiah taught Israel during the kingdom offer: reaffirming Torah's moral core and intensifying it beyond the letter, most fully in the Sermon on the Mount. This material continued even after Israel's leadership's verdict changed the material intent of His ministry (§III.D) — the exchanges of Holy Week draw on the same core — but its original address is Israel under the Torah of Moses, not the coming assembly; how it nonetheless reaches the assembly is Section V's own subject (§V.A–B).

A necessary caution belongs here before the survey begins, and it applies equally to Section V. All of Messiah's words carry final authority, but not everything the Gospels record Him saying or doing functions as a command, and treating narrative as command is a standing risk once a list like the one below exists to be mined for precedent. John 2 supplies the clearest test case. At the wedding in Cana, Yeshua tells the servants, "Fill the waterpots with water" (John 2:7)[^john27b], and the six stone jars set aside for Jewish purification become wine; John's own comment states what the account is for: "This beginning of his signs did Yeshua in Cana of Galilee, and manifested his glory; and his disciples believed on him" (2:11)[^john211]. The instruction is addressed to specific servants, within one narrative moment, in service of one sign — not a pattern for anyone to reenact. What follows below, in both this section and the next, is confined, so far as the survey can manage it, to instruction addressed generally rather than to a bystander within one scene, and tied to a stated purpose that reaches beyond the immediate narrative. Section VI.A returns to this same caution from the other side.

**A. Carried Over from the Torah of Moses.** Messiah reaffirms rather than replaces the moral core of Torah, in exchanges that continue through the last week of His life — the Temple debates of Holy Week are the culmination of this engagement with Israel's leaders, not incidental teaching alongside it (§III.D).
- Love YHWH your God with all your heart, soul, and mind (Matt. 22:37, quoting Deut. 6:5)[^matt2237]
- Love your neighbor as yourself (Matt. 22:39, quoting Lev. 19:18)[^matt2239]
- Honor your father and mother; do not murder, commit adultery, steal, or bear false witness (Matt. 15:4[^matt154]; 19:18–19[^matt1918])
- Worship and serve the Lord God only; do not put Him to the test (Matt. 4:7, 10)[^matt47]

**B. Intensified Beyond Moses.** Spoken in the Sermon on the Mount, before the hinge (§III.A) — the command is not new, but the bar is raised, for Israel under the kingdom offer.
- Anger toward a brother is treated as murder; be reconciled before you worship (Matt. 5:21–24)[^matt521]
- Lust is treated as adultery (Matt. 5:27–28)[^matt527]
- Love your enemies and pray for those who persecute you (Matt. 5:44)[^matt544] — beyond "love your neighbor"
- Do not resist the one who is evil; turn the other cheek (Matt. 5:39)[^matt539]

Section V takes up the second part of the Law of the Messiah: what carries this same material forward for the assembly, and what Messiah gave that Israel under the Law could not yet have received.

## V. Commands to the Church

The second part of the Law of the Messiah is what Messiah gave specifically for the age His own withdrawal from Israel's official offer opens (§III.C–D): confirmed, in part, by carrying Torah's substance forward through apostolic restatement rather than by Messiah's own direct original address to the coming assembly (A–B below); and, in part, spoken by Messiah Himself directly to that assembly's own founders, in the Upper Room, after the resurrection, and at the Great Commission (C–F below) — then confirmed and enumerated by the apostles in their own right (G below).

**A. Carried Over from the Torah of Moses, as Given to the Church.** Not by fresh direct address — the assembly did not yet exist to be addressed — but by apostolic restatement after Pentecost. Paul states the whole Decalogue's substance in a single line: "he that loves his neighbor has fulfilled the law... it is summed up in this word, namely, You shall love your neighbor as yourself... love therefore is the fulfilment of the law" (Rom. 13:8–10)[^rom1389]. James calls the same command "the royal law" (James 2:8)[^james28].

**B. Intensified, as Given to the Church.** Spoken by Messiah Himself, but privately, to His own disciples, after the hinge (§III) — not to the crowds under the kingdom offer.
- Forgive without limit — not seven times, but seventy times seven (Matt. 18:21–22)[^matt1821]
- Whoever would be first must be servant of all (Mark 9:35[^mark935]; 10:43–44[^mark1043])
- Deny yourself and take up your cross daily (Luke 9:23[^luke923], addressed to the disciples per its Matthean parallel, 16:24)
- Love one another *even as I have loved you* — the "new commandment" that intensifies neighbor-love into cross-shaped love, spoken in the Upper Room on the threshold of the withdrawal-period's close (John 13:34[^john1334]; 15:12[^john1512b])

**C. New to the Messianic Economy.** Post-resurrection and Upper Room material, addressed directly to the coming assembly age. Moses could not have given these; they presuppose the Messiah's death, resurrection, and the coming of the Spirit.
- Repent and believe the good news (Mark 1:15)[^mark115]; believe in God, believe also in Me (John 14:1)[^john141]
- Be immersed in the name of the Father, Son, and Holy Spirit (Matt. 28:19)[^matt2819b]
- Keep My commandments; abide in Me (John 14:15, 21[^john1415b]; 15:4, 10[^john154])
- Do this — the bread and the cup — in remembrance of Me (Luke 22:19)[^luke2219]
- Wait for the promised Spirit before you go (Luke 24:49[^luke2449]; Acts 1:4, 8[^acts14])
- Go into all the world and make disciples, teaching them to observe all I have commanded (Matt. 28:19–20)[^matt2819c]

**D. The High Priestly Prayer: Christ's Own Definition of the Assembly's Life Together.** John 17 stands apart from V.A–C in grammatical form — it is prayer, addressed to the Father, not a direct second-person imperative laid on the disciples — and it is treated here as such: not a fifth list of commands, but Messiah's own statement of what obedience to V.A–C's commands is *for*. It is nonetheless the single most extended discourse in the Gospels concerning the disciples, and it is explicitly not limited to the Twelve: "Neither for these only do I pray, but for them also that believe on me through their word" (17:20)[^john1720] names every subsequent believer as its object as plainly as any verse in the New Testament — spoken, like 13:34's summary command, in the same Upper Room, on the same night, after the withdrawal (§III) and immediately before the passion that opens the assembly age.

Four requests structure the prayer. *Unity*, first: "keep them in your name which you have given me, that they may be one, even as we are" (17:11)[^john1711], expanded — "that they may all be one; even as you, Father, are in me, and I in you, that they also may be in us: that the world may believe that you did send me" (17:21)[^john1721] — offered not as an end in itself but as public, evidential proof by which the world recognizes Messiah's sending, the same evidential logic Section III.A attached to the kingdom offer's own public miracles, now relocated to the disciples' corporate life together. *Sanctification through truth*, second: "Sanctify them in the truth: your word is truth" (17:17)[^john1717], for which Messiah consecrates Himself on their behalf: "for their sakes I sanctify myself, that they themselves also may be sanctified in truth" (17:19)[^john1719]. *Mission without removal*, third: "I pray not that you should take them from the world, but that you should keep them from the evil one... As you did send me into the world, even so sent I them into the world" (17:15, 18)[^john1715] — the disciples' posture toward the world is the same sent posture Messiah Himself held, not a withdrawal from it. *Shared love and glory*, fourth: "the glory which you have given me I have given to them; that they may be one, even as we are one" (17:22)[^john1722], so "that the love with which you loved me may be in them, and I in them" (17:26)[^john1726] — restating, in prayer, the very axiom of 13:34's command (§VII).

Read this way, John 17 does not add a new item to Sections V.A–C; it names their intended outcome — a people known by the same covenantal knowledge of God the Son Himself has (17:3)[^john173], set apart by the word rather than removed from the world, visibly one before a watching world, and marked by the Father's own love for the Son — held out, in advance of the fact, to "them also that believe... through their word" (17:20), which by the paper's own reckoning includes every reader this paper is written for. If Section V.B–C states what Messiah commands, this is the fullest statement in the Gospels of what He asked the Father to accomplish in those who keep it.

**E. What Was Set Aside.** Consistent with Fruchtenbaum's second observation, the Gospels and apostolic teaching are equally clear about what did *not* carry forward: dietary restriction (Mark 7:19)[^mark719] and sabbath-keeping as a binding ordinance (Col. 2:16–17)[^col216], with rest now found in Messiah Himself rather than a day (Matt. 11:28–30)[^matt1128]. This category matters as much as the others: a systematized "law of the Messiah" that quietly reinstates Mosaic particulars — or invents new ones with equivalent weight — has misread the very framework it claims to follow.

**F. "All That I Have Commanded": Matthew 28:20 as a Forward-Pointing Summary.** Matthew 28:20 does not hand a disciple a finished checklist; it hands the apostles a mandate to unpack one. Spoken after the withdrawal (§III), after "my assembly" had already been named (16:18), and immediately before Pentecost opens the Law of the Messiah's own era (§II), "all things whatever I commanded you" functions as Messiah's own forward reference to a body the apostles will spend the rest of the New Testament identifying, restating, and applying — Categories A–D above, together with Section IV's own material carried forward (§V.A), read and re-taught for an assembly Messiah's earthly ministry anticipated but did not yet address directly in every particular. What follows surveys the apostles doing exactly that.

**G. Confirmed and Enumerated by the Apostles.** If Matthew 28:20 makes "all things whatever I commanded you"[^matt2820c] the distinguishing mark of a disciple, the rest of the New Testament shows the apostles taking that mandate seriously — not by adding new revelation, but by restating, enumerating, and applying what Messiah had already commanded.

No book restates Messiah's commands more explicitly than **1 John**. John repeatedly ties present obedience back to Messiah's own words: "hereby we know that we know him, if we keep his commandments" (1 John 2:3)[^1john23]; "he that says he abides in him ought himself also to walk even as he walked" (2:6)[^1john26]; the command to love one another is "no new commandment," but "the old commandment which you all had from the beginning" and "the old commandment is the word which you all heard" (2:7–8)[^1john27] — a direct backward reference to John 13:34. Most strikingly, John collapses belief and obedience into a single command: "this is his commandment, that we should believe in the name of his Son Yeshua Messiah, and love one another, even as he gave us commandment" (1 John 3:23)[^1john323]. The letter closes on the same note: "his commandments are not grievous" (5:3)[^1john53]. **2 John** repeats it almost verbatim: "not as though I wrote to you a new commandment, but that which we had from the beginning, that we love one another. And this is love, that we should walk after his commandments" (vv. 5–6)[^2john5]. This is the strongest New Testament evidence that the apostles understood discipleship precisely as this paper defines it: obedience to an identifiable set of commands traceable to Messiah Himself.

Elsewhere, **James** calls love of neighbor "the royal law" (James 2:8)[^james28b] and insists disciples be "doers of the word, and not hearers only" (1:22)[^james122]. **Paul** names the category directly — "bear you all one another's burdens, and so fulfil the Torah of Messiah" (Gal. 6:2)[^gal62b] — and elsewhere distinguishes commands he has *from the Lord* from his own apostolic judgment: "to the married I give charge, indeed not I, but the Lord" (1 Cor. 7:10)[^1cor710], against "concerning virgins I have no commandment of the Lord: but I give my judgment" (7:25)[^1cor725]; he tells the Thessalonians, "you all know what charge we gave you through the Lord Yeshua" (1 Thess. 4:2)[^1thess42]. Paul marking his own distinction between what Messiah commanded and what is his own apostolic judgment is the same distinction Section VI.A takes up directly, extended to the whole body of occasional apostolic instruction that distinction makes room for.

## VI. What Is Not a Command: Textual Example, Christian Liberty, and Personal Conviction

If the Law of the Messiah is a definable, closed set of commands (§IV, §V), confirmed and enumerated by apostolic restatement (§V.A, §V.G), then everything else — concrete narrative and occasional instruction that teaches by example rather than by legislation, the disputable matters, the personal disciplines, the methods by which believers serve one another in a given time and place — falls outside that set. This is not a deficiency to be filled in by church tradition, denominational preference, or discipleship-program methodology elevated to the status of command. Scripture supplies three categories for this remainder, which discipleship teaching regularly runs together: textual example, addressed to a particular audience and circumstance rather than legislated for every age; the corporate exercise of Christian liberty in love; and personal conviction proper, binding on the individual conscience alone. Section IV's caution about John 2 opened the first; Paul develops the second at length; James and Paul together supply the third.

**A. Textual Example of Practical Living.** Scripture records a great deal that is concrete, authoritative, and true about Messiah's own ministry and the apostles' pastoral instruction — without thereby being a command laid on every disciple in every age. Section IV's caution already named the paradigm case: at the wedding in Cana, Yeshua tells the servants to fill the stone waterpots with water (John 2:7)[^john27c], and John states plainly what the account is for — Messiah "manifested his glory; and his disciples believed on him" (2:11)[^john211b]. Modern readers rightly learn of His glory, grace, and provision through the account; treating the instruction itself as a reenactable pattern misreads what kind of text it is.

The same caution applies, differently, to a large body of apostolic instruction addressed to a particular first-century congregation and circumstance — most extensively in 1 and 2 Corinthians. Much of that material — the instructions on food offered to idols in chapters 8–10, on head coverings and the Lord's Supper in chapter 11, on the collection in chapter 16, the specific relational counsel throughout 2 Corinthians — was the certain, authoritative word of God to that congregation, addressed to circumstances (an idol-saturated market economy, a specific factional dispute, a specific relief offering for Jerusalem) that do not recur identically for every modern reader. These passages are not less inspired for being occasional; Scripture is Scripture whether it legislates for every age or answers one congregation's particular question, and they are to be held in the highest regard as the direct word of God to their original hearers.

But their binding form for a modern disciple, like Cana's, is not "replicate the instruction's literal particulars" — it is the principle the instruction embodies. Paul's own summary of the idol-meat instructions states the transferable content directly: "'All things are lawful'; but not all things are expedient. 'All things are lawful'; but not all things edify. Let no man seek his own, but each his neighbor's good" (1 Cor. 10:23–24)[^1cor1023]. The head-covering and Lord's-Supper instructions of chapter 11 embody order and consideration for the whole body; the collection instructions of chapter 16 embody generosity gauged to what "he may prosper" (16:2)[^1cor162]. The modern reader does not inherit a fixed covering-halakhah, a fixed collection schedule, or a recipe for filling stone jars; through concrete cases — one from Messiah's own ministry, one from apostolic pastoral instruction — the reader inherits the underlying pattern of walking by faith and by love that Sections VI.B–C take up directly. This is not a demotion of either text — it is the highest possible regard for each as what it actually is: authoritative textual example, teaching a principle through a concrete circumstance rather than adding a new item to the closed set surveyed in Sections IV–V. The axiom underneath every such example remains the one already named in Section V.B: "love one another, even as I have loved you" (John 13:34)[^john1334b] — a rule the New Testament illustrates repeatedly but does not, and could not, exhaustively catalog for every circumstance a disciple will meet.

**B. Christian Liberty in Love.** **Romans 14:1–15:7** addresses disputed matters — food, calendar observance — with a consistent instruction: welcome one another, but "let each man be fully assured in his own mind" (14:5)[^rom145], because "the kingdom of God is not eating and drinking, but righteousness and peace and joy in the Holy Spirit" (14:17)[^rom1417]. The strong are told to bear with the weak and not to please themselves, but to build up their neighbor (15:1–2)[^rom151] — not to bind their own conviction on others as though it were law.

**1 Corinthians 8–10** extends the same logic to food offered to idols (§VI.A). Paul's summary principle — "'All things are lawful,' but not all things are helpful. 'All things are lawful,' but not all things build up. Let no one seek his own good, but the good of his neighbor" (10:23–24)[^1cor1023b] — is not a rule about idol meat alone; it is a method for handling everything Messiah did not command. Paul's own practice illustrates it: though he had a right to material support as an apostle (9:14)[^1cor914], he waived it for the sake of the good news (9:15, 18)[^1cor915], becoming "all things to all men, that I may by all means save some" (9:22)[^1cor922]. Freedom, exercised through self-control, in service of others, and to God's glory (10:31)[^1cor1031] — not personal license and not imposed obligation — is the pattern.

**Galatians 5:13** states the principle most compactly: "you all were called for freedom... only use not your freedom for an occasion to the flesh, but through love be servants one to another"[^gal513].

**C. Personal Conviction and the Measure of Faith.** Romans 14 itself supplies the sharper category underneath Christian liberty. Paul does not resolve the disputed matters of diet and calendar into one correct practice binding on every conscience; he tells the assembly to let "each man be fully assured in his own mind" (14:5)[^rom145b]. The measure by which that assurance is reached is itself personal: God has "dealt to each man a measure of faith" (Rom. 12:3)[^rom123], and the Spirit's own gifts are distributed by the Spirit "dividing to each one severally even as He will" (1 Cor. 12:11)[^1cor1211] — the Spirit does not work identically in every believer, and a conviction genuinely given by the Spirit to one believer is not thereby a universal law for another believer to whom the Spirit has not given the same conviction. 1 Corinthians 8 states the same lesson from the conscience's own side: "there is not in all men that knowledge: but some, being used until now to the idol, eat as of a thing sacrificed to an idol; and their conscience being weak is defiled" (8:7)[^1cor87] — the same act, the same information available to both, and yet a genuinely different conviction, because the Spirit forms conscience believer by believer, not by uniform decree.

James states the resulting obligation with the same bluntness the rest of his letter uses throughout: "to him therefore that knows to do good, and does it not, to him it is sin" (James 4:17)[^james417]. Read against Romans 14, this is not a general maxim about moral knowledge in the abstract; it is the specific claim that a conviction the Spirit has actually given a believer is binding on that believer's own conscience — to act against it is sin for them — while another believer, walking by a different measure of faith on the very same question, may act differently with a clear conscience before God. Paul states the same rule from the other side: "the faith which you have, have you to yourself before God... whatever is not of faith is sin" (Rom. 14:22–23)[^rom1422]. This is the category personal discipline, cultural conviction, and much of what discipleship programs teach as practice actually occupies: not commands of Messiah binding on every disciple alike (§IV, §V), and not bare preference either, but Spirit-given conviction — real, and personally binding precisely because it is personal, and never rightly exported as law to a brother whose own faith the Spirit has measured differently.

Spiritual disciplines, discipleship curricula, ministry philosophies, cultural applications, and personal convictions are not thereby illegitimate — Paul plainly commends exercising them in love, and James plainly holds a believer accountable to the convictions the Spirit has actually given them. But all of it is categorically different from the commands of Messiah surveyed in Sections IV and V. It is how a disciple already obedient to Messiah's actual commands chooses, in a given context and under the Spirit's own leading, to serve others; it is not itself an addition to the Law of the Messiah, and teaching it as though it carried the weight of explicit Scriptural command tends toward Pharisaism — building a fence around the law that Scripture itself never erected.

## VII. Reconnecting Disciple-Making to the Commands of Messiah

The Great Commission's grammar (§I), the two-phase shape of Messiah's own ministry (§III), the Law of the Messiah's framework (§II), and the two parts of Messiah's commands it yields — what Israel received under the kingdom offer (§IV) and what the assembly received directly and through apostolic confirmation (§V) — converge on a single, testable claim: a disciple is someone being taught to observe a definite, identifiable body of Messiah's commands — not a generalized program of spiritual growth, and not a bundle of methods, disciplines, and cultural convictions treated as though they carried equal authority.

One command sits over the whole of it as its own summary, and the New Testament treats it that way. Messiah names it Himself, in the Upper Room, on the threshold of the withdrawal period's close: "love one another, even as I have loved you" (John 13:34)[^john1334c]. John calls it, at once, "no new commandment" and a "new commandment" (1 John 2:7–8)[^1john27b] — old, because it restates Leviticus 19:18's love of neighbor, taught to Israel under the kingdom offer (§IV.A) and carried forward for the assembly by apostolic restatement (§V.A); new, because it is now measured by Messiah's own cross-shaped love rather than by the self as the standard (§V.B). Every category this paper has distinguished serves that one axiom differently: the commands of Sections IV and V state what love requires, for Israel and for the assembly in turn; the textual examples of Section VI.A show what love looked like both in Messiah's own ministry and in one first-century congregation's actual circumstances, to be studied rather than mechanically copied; Christian liberty (§VI.B) is love's refusal to bind on another what has not in fact been bound by Messiah; personal conviction (§VI.C) is love's Spirit-given application to a circumstance Scripture does not individually name.

This reframes the task of disciple-making concretely. The core curriculum of discipleship is teaching believers to *know* and to *do* what is catalogued in Sections IV and V — commands with textual warrant in the words of Messiah Himself and their apostolic restatement, rightly distinguished by which part of the Law of the Messiah each belongs to (§III.E). Section VI.A's textual examples remain good, Spirit-led, and worth studying closely as illustrations of that same axiom under real circumstances, whether drawn from Messiah's own recorded ministry or from apostolic pastoral instruction. Everything in the rest of Section VI — the disputable matters, the personal disciplines, the methods by which believers serve one another in their particular circumstances — remains good, Spirit-led, and worth practicing, but is taught and received as liberty and personal conviction in service to others (1 Cor. 10:33)[^1cor1033], never imposed as if Messiah had commanded it.

## VIII. Conclusion

The American church has no shortage of disciple-making programs; what this paper contends it lacks is a shared, textually grounded account of what a disciple is actually commanded to observe — and a working distinction between that command set, the textual examples that illustrate it, and the personal conviction the Spirit gives variously to each believer along the way. Israel had 613 commandments and knew them as such. The assembly inherits a smaller, more concentrated body of commands from her Messiah — Torah reaffirmed and intensified before the hinge, and made new after it (§III) — together with an apostolic witness, especially in 1 John, that treats obedience to those commands as inseparable from knowing Him at all. Recovering that body of commands, distinguishing it clearly from textual example, Christian liberty, and personal conviction, and teaching disciples to observe it, is not a departure from the Great Commission. It is what the Great Commission, on its own terms, already asked for.
